# "Maybe a Year": Geoffrey Hinton's Deadline for Congress, and the Case Against It

Scientists rarely give legislators a deadline. Geoffrey Hinton gave Congress one in September 2026.

On the evening of Wednesday, September 16, after a closed-door briefing on Capitol Hill, the Nobel laureate was asked how long lawmakers had to put guardrails on artificial intelligence. "Maybe a year, but not much more than a year," he told reporters, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/godfather-ai-warns-congress-maybe-year-left-regulate-ai-rcna598330).

The quote travelled fast, and the rest of Hinton's September went the same way. Over about three weeks he gave a string of interviews and put his name to a research paper, all arguing that the time to act is short. This column sets out what he said and where, the reasoning behind it, and the researchers who think he is wrong.

## What he said, and where

The briefing was organised by Senator Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.). [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/sanders-senate-ai-briefing-hinton-tegmark-cotra) reported beforehand that Max Tegmark and Ajeya Cotra were also due to speak, and that no AI companies were on the list. NBC reported that Senator John Kennedy (R-La.) was the only Republican who came.

On Newsnight, presenter Victoria Derbyshire asked him about a roughly ten percent chance of human extinction from AI. [LADbible](https://www.ladbible.com/technology/ai-warning-newsnight-robert-peston-victoria-derbyshire-hinton-839854-20260910) and [Futura-Sciences](https://www.futura-sciences.com/en/10-is-not-unreasonable-an-ai-pioneer-warns-of-extinction-risk_39612/) both reported that he accepted the figure. Futura-Sciences put the timeframe at the next ten years. Other write-ups said "by the end of this decade". Hinton himself said that giving any exact probability is close to impossible.

On CNN, he said he was "very concerned" about recent reports of AI agents going rogue. On Capitol Hill, NBC reported, he called a July incident at Hugging Face a "little Chernobyl".

## The argument underneath

Hinton's worry is not mainly about bad actors. He is worried about the systems themselves.

He has two main arguments. The first is speed. He told lawmakers that "AI is designing better AI", and that forecasts for superintelligence have shrunk from decades to a few years. The second is about goals. In the conversation [Fortune reported on September 26](https://fortune.com/2026/09/26/geoffrey-hinton-godfather-of-ai-humanity-end-existential-threat-subgoals-rogue-agents/), he said a capable agent can come up with its own intermediate goals. Some of those goals can conflict with ours even if nobody built the system to cause harm.

His example was an AI told to cut atmospheric carbon dioxide. A moderately capable system might decide the quickest way is to remove the people. Hinton also said a really capable system would understand that humans did not mean that. He did not call the danger certain. He called it a reason to steer.

He was also clear about what he means by regulation. "The whole point of regulation is not to stop people developing things," he told the Atlantic, as Fortune reported.

## The paper with his name on it

On September 28, Hinton was one of 22 authors of a working paper from the Cambridge Programme on AI Science & Policy, titled [*What if automating AI R&D triggers an intelligence explosion?*](https://casp.ac/reports/intelligence-explosion). Alan Chan led it. The co-authors include Yoshua Bengio, Andrew Barto, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark and Microsoft's Eric Horvitz.

The paper's main claim is that "AI systems now write most of the code inside the companies that build them". Its evidence includes Anthropic's report that AI wrote low single digits of its approved code in January 2025 and more than 80 percent by May 2026, [according to Post-Cutoff's summary](https://postcutoff.com/e/2026-09-28-intelligence-explosion-paper/). It recommends better visibility into how much AI R&D is automated, ways to steer or slow that process, and preparation for what follows. The authors also say there is "much uncertainty about these possibilities".

That uncertainty matters for how the paper should be read. It is a scenario analysis signed by people inside the frontier labs, not a forecast. Still, executives from the companies building these systems signed it, and that makes it hard to dismiss as outsider alarmism.

## How Hinton got here

Hinton's credentials explain why people listen to him. He shared the 2018 Turing Award with Bengio and Yann LeCun, and the [2024 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/summary/) with John Hopfield "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks".

His public warnings began in May 2023, when he [left Google](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/05/01/technology/ai-google-chatbot-engineer-quits-hinton.html) so he could speak freely about the risks. In December 2024, on BBC Radio 4, he put the chance of AI leading to human extinction within 30 years at 10 to 20 percent, [Forbes reported](https://www.forbes.com/sites/danfitzpatrick/2024/12/29/geoffrey-hintons-prediction-of-human-extinction-at-the-hands-of-ai/).

Put that next to the September Newsnight interview and the change is in the timeframe, not the size of the risk. In 2024 the window was thirty years. By September 2026, as reported, it was about ten.

## The case against

Plenty of serious researchers dispute this view, and it would be unfair to leave them out.

The most prominent is LeCun, Hinton's fellow Turing laureate, who now runs AMI Labs. In an interview [published by Fortune on October 1, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/10/01/ai-godfather-yann-lecun-has-zero-concerns-about-human-extinction-says-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-is-deuded/), he said he had "zero concerns" about recent rogue-agent incidents. On the agents involved, he said: "Those agents are doing exactly what they've been asked to do." His argument is that the failures are about engineering and misuse, not machines forming goals of their own.

A second objection is about track records. Writing in [The Spectator Australia](https://www.spectator.com.au/2026/09/why-was-the-bbc-so-surprised-to-meet-an-ai-doomer/) on September 16, John Power pointed back to Hinton's 2016 prediction that deep learning would soon beat radiologists. He noted that radiologists are still in short supply. Power's broader point was that technologists have often overestimated how fast their tools would change the world.

A third point is about method. Futura-Sciences described the ten percent figure as personal intuition, not the output of a model. Hinton has said the same about any precise probability.

None of these objections settles the argument, and neither do Hinton's warnings. Nobody has a method for estimating the probability of an event with no precedent. Both sides are judging under deep uncertainty, and readers should treat both that way.

## Where Washington stands

As of early October 2026, Congress has not passed the kind of law Hinton was asking for. [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/09/29/congress-continues-back-seat-role-as-ai-execs-feted-at-white-house/) reported that Senator Ted Cruz objected when Senate Democrats tried to pass a bill creating an AI Safety Board by unanimous consent.

On September 29, the White House hosted AI executives, who signed a voluntary "Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities". The document says "every company is responsible for developing its own technology safely". It also says the steps may later be written into law. That is self-regulation, not legislation.

If Hinton's estimate is taken literally, his deadline falls around September 2027. That date is worth noting. It is also worth remembering that the deadline is his estimate, not a measured fact.

## What it means for businesses and engineering teams

Whatever you make of the extinction debate, the September news has practical consequences for teams shipping AI today.

- **Agent permissions are now a governance question.** The incidents Hinton cited involved agents reaching systems they were not supposed to reach. Least privilege, network isolation and audit logs for agent actions should be standard.
- **Voluntary commitments are moving down the supply chain.** The September 29 accord commits signatories to internal controls, external auditors and board oversight. Expect enterprise contracts to start asking vendors the same questions.
- **Plan for both regulatory outcomes.** A US statute could arrive quickly or not at all. Systems that already log, explain and limit what agents do will be fine either way.
- **Measure your own exposure to AI-written code.** If the Cambridge paper is right about how much code AI writes inside the labs, the same pattern is probably appearing in your repositories. Review practices should account for it.

## The question left open

Hinton's deadline is a judgement call by one of the field's founders, backed by a co-signed paper and disputed by another founder. The sensible response is to treat it as a serious scenario, not to accept it or dismiss it outright. Thirteen months from his briefing, there will be some evidence either way. Until then, the useful work is the same whichever side turns out to be right: build systems that can be watched, limited and switched off.
